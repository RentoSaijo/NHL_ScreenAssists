The path from a replay to a ranking

Whose screen helps the shot?

The useful question is more specific than who stands near the crease. We follow the scoring shot, locate the defending goalie, and ask which teammates occupy the goalie-to-puck sightline at a relevant moment.

01 · Resolve the shot

Match the goal to tracking. Normalize the attacking direction and estimate release and arrival from the puck path.

02 · Measure obstruction

Describe proximity, duration, crossings, and the time between an obstruction and release.

03 · Attribute a credit

Apply a frozen detector and two thresholds chosen against independent player annotations.

A screen assist has a precise counting rule

Each qualifying offensive teammate receives one estimated screen assist on a goal. We exclude the credited scorer. Multiple teammates can qualify, and a player’s official assist does not prevent a screen-assist credit. Empty nets and shootouts are outside the statistic.

The conservative definition uses a threshold selected with precision-weighted F0.5. The broader definition uses F1, with its threshold constrained to include every conservative credit. These are two operating definitions; neither column is a confidence-interval endpoint. A brief crossing can qualify when its geometry and timing resemble annotated screens.

The shot comes first

Public NHL replays contain short sequences around goals. We use their observed 10 Hz timestamps, not an assumed timestamp for the final frame. Players are tracked by NHL identifiers across changing slots. Rotating coordinates puts the attacking goal at x = 89 ft for every replay.

Candidate arrivals cross the goal plane near the mouth of the net. For ordinary releases, we look backward for puck movement toward goal, separation from the scorer, and agreement with the official shot location. For tips and deflections, we trace the preceding flight and check the scorer near the terminal deflection. Rebound goals are anchored to the scorer’s current attempt; a screen on a previous shot does not automatically receive credit.

These are estimates from planar tracking. Clips with unresolved timing, missing critical frames, or an unidentified goalie remain unevaluable. We retain a release sensitivity of ±0.10 s, and we avoid interpolating across a gap longer than 0.21 s in the analysis window.

Use a finite sightline

Let g, p, and s denote the goalie, puck, and potential screener’s tracked positions. The projection below tells us whether the screener lies between the goalie and puck. Perpendicular distance alone would also count players behind the goalie or beyond the puck.

u = (s − g) · (p − g) / ‖p − g‖²
d = ‖(s − g) − u(p − g)‖   when 0 < u < 1

We summarize this geometry during the 0.50 s before release, a release band from −0.20 to +0.10 s, and the puck’s flight toward the goalie. Post-release windows stop at arrival and exclude positions more than 1 ft beyond the goalie. Features include obstruction duration, continuous duration, minimum separation, goalie proximity, side-to-side crossings, and the interval available to reacquire the puck. The baseline combines proximity-weighted alignment with duration and reacquisition time.

Shoulder position is an uncertainty, not a body model

The player sensor sits on the right shoulder. The goalie sensor is also displaced from the eyes. The public feeds do not supply body orientation, gaze, stance, or puck height. A skating direction cannot recover those missing measurements: players move backward, turn sideways, and remain stationary.

We therefore treat the sightline as a planar proxy. The primary geometry uses an effective 1.50 ft tolerance, with 1.00 and 2.00 ft alternatives to examine sensitivity to body extent and sensor displacement. These bounded scenarios change the distance-based obstruction summaries while keeping the fitted detector and attribution thresholds fixed. They do not identify every physically possible posture or establish that a goalie actually loses sight of the puck.

In the explorer, the band can be hidden to inspect the tracked points alone. Advance one frame at a time near release and notice how a small change in either timing or alignment can affect a marginal screen.

Learn from independent annotations

We construct a private reference set from reviewed, quality-controlled Clear Sight Analytics records. Matching checks the NHL game, scorer, teams, goalie, period, and clock, even when an imported event identifier is available. Duplicate participant memberships are removed.

Positive examples require an explicit offensive or mixed screen classification and a named offensive teammate. Negative examples require an explicit clear-sight or defensive-only classification with no offensive teammate recorded. Screen-on-pass cases and screen-classified events without a named offensive teammate are excluded. Within a participant-bearing screen event, the recorded participant set serves as the attribution reference; completeness of that set remains an assumption.

We develop on 2023–24, select the detector and thresholds on 2024–25, and reserve 2025–26 for final evaluation. A geometric baseline competes with ridge logistic regression using public geometric features. Player identity and CSA scoring probabilities are never predictors. Selection uses player-level average precision, with the baseline preferred in a tie.

Performance is reported separately for detecting a screened goal and identifying its participants. The classifier’s calibration describes the selected reference population. It should not be read as a universal probability that an NHL player screens on any shot. View held-out results →

Put every denominator on the same footing

Screen assists / game = credits / official appearances
Screen assists / 60 min = 3,600 × credits / ice-time seconds

Every numerator and denominator uses the same season, competition, team, and October 19, 2026 cutoff. A traded player contributes appearances, ice time, and credits to the team represented in that game. Players with zero detected credits are included. Counts have no exposure minimum; rate rankings start at five games or 60 minutes and allow adjustment.

We use full official exposure rather than inventing replacement credits for unresolved clips. As a result, rates measure detected screen assists per actual exposure and can be depressed by missing or unevaluable tracking. Small samples, playing roles, teammates, and team shooting volume also affect rankings.

Reproduce the snapshot

The repository contains original R preparation and analysis code, explicit detector parameters, public goal–player credits, official player-game exposure, short replay segments, and aggregate research results. Full tracking caches stay local. CSA records and event-level reference labels are held in memory and are not distributed. Re-estimating CSA-dependent models requires authorized database access; the published parameters and aggregates support public reconstruction of the rankings and report.
